# Worked exchanges and voice edits

All dialogue below is original and fictional, with adult participants. These examples teach decisions, not lines to reuse indiscriminately. Techniques T01–T08 are in techniques.md; the branches and editing method are practical judgments.

## 1. A playful profile opener with three possible continuations
Profile: “Ambitious spreadsheets for unambitious holidays.”
User's voice: short, dry, no emojis. User actually prefers loose plans.

**Send:** “How many tabs does doing nothing require?”
**Alternative, sincere:** “What's your ideal do-nothing holiday?”
**Alternative, warmer:** “An organised approach to relaxing. Where are you heading next?”
**Learn:** Exaggerate the implication already in the prompt; don't invent a personality.

If she joins: “Six tabs. Minimum. What's your system?”
**Send:** “One vague idea and a weather check. I may need supervision.”
This uses the known loose-planning preference. It offers a small role without demanding it.

If she answers literally: “It just keeps the bookings together.”
**Send:** “Fair. Where are you planning next?”
She has supplied ordinary meaning. Follow it instead of explaining the joke.

If she objects: “Why does everyone make fun of that?”
**Send:** “Sorry—I meant it fondly, but I see how it came across.”
Do not insist that the tease was harmless. Let her choose whether to continue.

## 2. Build from what she adds, then stop performing
Her: “I got lost on the walk. Found a good bakery though.”
User has not told us their hiking ability.
**Send:** “Strong argument for getting lost. What did you find?”
Her: “Cardamom buns. Worth every wrong turn.”
**Send:** “That's a very convincing route review.”
A statement can be a complete turn; no compulsory second question.

Her: “Honestly I needed the walk. Work's been rough.”
**Send:** “Sorry it's been rough. Want to tell me what's been going on?”
The emotional meaning changed. Do not keep the bakery riff running.

If she instead says “I'll send you the location,” receive the offer: “Please do.” Do not manufacture a date invitation if the user only wanted a reply.

## 3. Add interest without a sudden persona change
Both have enjoyed a discussion about restoring a chair. She explains why she kept its crooked leg.
User genuinely likes her thoughtfulness and wants to meet.
**Warm:** “I like that you kept the bit with a story.”
**Playful:** “The crooked leg has clearly negotiated permanent residency.”
**Direct:** “I like how you think. I'd enjoy hearing more over coffee.”

These alternatives do different jobs. Pick according to the user's intent and voice; do not stack all three. There is no evidence that a flattering line proves mutual attraction.

## 4. Invitation branches that remain concrete
Known: both like independent cinemas. User wants to meet, but no schedule is supplied.
**Send:** “I'd like to continue this in person. Fancy a film and a drink sometime?”
For a first meeting with easier conversation, coffee can be proposed instead; consider their preferences.

“Yes, Thursday?”
Ask the user if Thursday works before producing an acceptance. An optional draft must keep that condition visible outside the copy-ready message.

“Maybe after my deadline next week?”
**Send:** “Of course. Let's leave it until after the deadline.”
If they explicitly invite a check-in, use that. Otherwise leave room rather than converting “maybe” into a booking.

“Not ready to meet yet, but I'm enjoying talking.”
**Send:** “That's fine. I'm enjoying it too.”
Use only if true for the user. Continue normally; do not punish the boundary with silence.

“I don't think we're a match.”
**Send:** “Thanks for being honest. Take care.”
No persuasive alternative.

## 5. Make the reply sound like the user
Voice samples: “Fair point.” “That sounds good.” “I'm more of a last-minute person.”
Her: “I booked the whole trip before breakfast.”
Overwritten draft: “Your logistical prowess has left me both intimidated and enchanted; I hereby submit my application for your next adventure.”

**Edit:** “Before breakfast? I barely manage coffee.”
Only use if consistent with the user's actual habits; otherwise: “Before breakfast? That's efficient.”
**More direct:** “I like your decisiveness.”
The edit removes inflated praise, fake bureaucracy, and an uninvited role on her trip. It retains the user's short sentence rhythm. Don't add emojis when none are used.

If the user wants more warmth than their samples, change that explicitly. Voice matching is a starting point, not a permanent identity.

## 6. Give the literal answer when it is owed
Her: “What do you actually do for work?”
Unknown job: ask the user for their job; do not invent an answer in a ready-to-send block.
Known job and specialty: civil engineer working on transport projects.
**Send:** “I'm a civil engineer—mostly transport projects.”
Optional playful addition, if accurate: “A lot of arguing with roads on a screen.”
Her “actually” may mean she wants a real answer after earlier jokes. Don't add another evasive character or secrecy bit.

## 7. Date preparation and review
User: “I talk too quickly when nervous.”
**Before:** Choose comfortable clothes, confirm the plan, and rehearse one short true story. Notice where a thought ends.
**During:** Finish the thought and leave space. If you lose your thread, “I've started that in the middle” can be an ordinary reset.
**Practice:** Say the story once as usual, then once with comfortable pauses. Which version felt like you and was easier to follow? These are options to try, not a proven comparative technique.

Afterwards: “She laughed a lot, but I felt I had to entertain her.”
**Read:** Laughter was present; your experience of performing matters too.
**Next:** If you want another meeting, try a quieter setting and see whether curiosity is mutual. If you did not enjoy the connection, you do not need to earn a second date.
**Learn:** Shared humor and mutual suitability are separate questions. Don't diagnose her from either observation.
